Term Limits: Who Wrote, Paid for, and Benefited from Measure W?

In 1996, La Palma voters capped City Council service at two consecutive four-year terms, followed by a mandatory four-year break. In 2019, sitting council members considered extending that limit; residents packed a meeting to oppose it, and the council tabled the idea 5-0. Five years later, essentially the same proposal returned as Measure W — this time backed by a taxpayer-funded outreach contract the City signed weeks before any public vote, and by a campaign committee run from a councilmember's own home and financed largely by sitting council members and their families. Measure W passed in November 2024 with about 58% of the vote. Its ballot language never disclosed that prior service wouldn't count toward the new cap — meaning some sitting members could now serve up to 20 consecutive years without a break.

What Happened to the Measure JJ Pension Payoff Promise?

In 2016, La Palma voters approved a permanent 1% sales tax after the campaign promised it would pay off the City's pension debt in nine years, save $20 million, and eventually lower other taxes. Nine years later, none of that happened. The City's own audited numbers show the unfunded pension liability grew from about $18.2 million to $23.6 million — a 29.8% increase — and CalPERS' most recent valuation puts the combined debt at $25.3 million, with a payoff date around 2040, roughly three decades after voters were promised nine years. In August 2025, Nitesh Patel told the council from the dais that the debt had “gone down.” The audited records say the opposite, and no one on the council corrected the record that night.

The Cases City Hall Can’t Ignore

The City is currently defending three lawsuits: two employment cases involving former City employees, and a wrongful-death case arising from a multi-agency police pursuit. In the first, a police officer's discrimination complaints went undocumented and uninvestigated by the department; a jury later found the City liable and awarded $8.4 million — 112 times what the City had offered to settle. The City is appealing. Four months after the verdict, a second former employee sued the City, alleging unpaid wages and failures by management to address her concerns. Separately, the City faces a wrongful-death lawsuit after a December 2024 police pursuit ended in a fatal collision at a red light — the heirs' pre-suit government claims sought $30 million — a case that tests whether the City's own pursuit policy met the legal standard required for immunity. The council has not committed to an independent investigation of any of it.

What's Happened to City Leadership?

Over the past decade, La Palma's three most important administrative roles — City Manager, Police Chief, and City Attorney — have changed hands, frequently through internal promotion rather than an open, competitive search. The City has had three city managers, one of whom resigned without the City ever issuing its own standalone announcement of it. The Police Department has cycled through four chiefs in about 26 months, including two officers internally promoted to chief who worked one final year before retirement, raising the compensation their pensions were based on. The City Attorney's firm changed with no record of a competitive review. In 2025, the council selected former Councilmember Peter Kim as City Manager from a field of 87 applicants.

Why Residents Couldn't Watch Council Meetings?

In 2020, the City Council cut its regular public meetings in half, citing COVID — and never restored the original schedule. For the next several years, residents repeatedly asked the council to livestream video of its meetings; the council majority said no every time, even to a proposal that would have simply researched the cost. By 2023, La Palma was one of only two Orange County cities without video access, and received a 'D' grade for accessibility from a Chapman University study. The council refused repeatedly to implement live video streaming and only reversed course in 2026, after a new state law removed its ability to choose otherwise — and even then, the City announced its promised system was delayed until October just days before the meeting where it was supposed to debut.
