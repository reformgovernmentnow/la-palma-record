Five Things La Palma Residents Should Know

Five major issues. One documented record. Start with the key facts, then follow the evidence behind each one.

01

Pension Debt: What Happened to the Nine-Year Payoff

In 2016, voters were told Measure JJ would help pay off the City's pension debt in nine years and save $20 million.

Today, CalPERS reports $25.3 million in unfunded pension liability, with minimum-payment projections extending to roughly 2040–41.

How did a nine-year payoff become nearly three decades?

Review the Pension Debt
02

Term Limits: Who Wrote, Paid for, and Benefited from Measure W?

Measure W increased La Palma's consecutive-service limit from 8 years to 12 while excluding prior service from the new calculation.

City-paid consultants helped draft ballot materials, and campaign filings show sitting council members and their families helped finance the campaign.

Who shaped the measure, who paid to support it, and who benefited?

03

Litigation: The Cases City Hall Can't Ignore

A police officer's discrimination complaints went undocumented and uninvestigated. A jury later awarded him $8.4 million for discrimination, retaliation, and failure to prevent discrimination. The City is appealing.

Two additional lawsuits involve a fatal police pursuit and alleged unpaid employee wages from a beloved Tiny Tots Program Director.

What happened and what did sworn testimony reveal?

Explore the Litigation Record
04

Leadership: A Decade of Turnover at City Hall

La Palma has had four police chiefs in roughly 26 months. One rose from sergeant to captain to chief and retired in just 25 months.

City Manager and other leadership has changed as well, raising questions about recruitment, continuity, and oversight.

What drove the turnover and how were positions filled?

Examine the Leadership Record
05

Transparency: Why Residents Couldn't Watch Council Meetings Online

In 2023, the Council rejected a proposal to research video livestreaming of its meetings. A month after adopting a new two-member agenda rule, another video proposal failed for lack of a second.

Video access moved forward in 2026 after new state law required remote public access.

Why did livestreaming take three years and a new state law to implement?
