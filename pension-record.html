Measure JJ and the Pension Debt

Measure JJ promised a nine-year pension payoff in 2016. The audited numbers, and what a sitting official told the council in 2025.

Covers the 2016 pension-tax promises and the one point in the record where those promises were checked against the City's own audited numbers.

November 2016 — The Council places Measure JJ, a permanent 1% sales tax, on the ballot. Opponents call it a “pension tax.” Proponents — including sitting council members — promise it will pay off the pension debt in 9 years, save $20 million, rebuild reserves, establish a pension stabilization fund, restore services, and lower the Utility Users Tax. Voters approve it. The City's unfunded pension liability (UAL) at the time: approximately $18.2 million (FY 2016-17).

August 5, 2025 — A resident asks the council for the current UAL. The Interim City Manager can't answer. Then-Mayor Pro Tem Nitesh Patel says from the dais it has “gone down” due to discretionary payments. The City's own audited reports show the opposite: UAL rose from $18.2 million (FY 2016-17) to $23.6 million (FY 2023-24) — up 29.8% — while the required annual contribution also grew. No colleague corrects the record. The nine-year payoff promised to voters would have come due that same year, and neither staff nor the council has ever publicly reconciled promise against result.

The campaign's own chart — The 2016 Yes on Measure JJ committee published its own forecast, “What Fiscally Sound Looks Like,” projecting a swing from a $309,200 deficit to a $1.8 million surplus by FY 2025-26, a General Fund balance climbing from roughly $10.2 million (FY 2016-17) to $14.3 million (FY 2026-27), and the promise that “with Measure JJ ending the depletion of reserves, the city has a plan to take that emergency fund, pay off the debt in 9 years and save the city $20 million.” The projected surplus was earmarked for rebuilding reserves, a Pension Stabilization Fund, capital needs, and eventually lowering the Utility Users Tax.

The promise, attributed to the City Manager by name — The committee's page names City Manager Laurie Murray as the official who would recommend directing the FY 2025-26 surplus toward reserves, services, and the Utility Users Tax. Murray retired effective December 19, 2019 — see Leadership Transitions — nearly six years before the fiscal year the campaign's own chart said she would make that recommendation.

CalPERS' June 30, 2024 actuarial valuation — CalPERS' most recent valuations for the City's Safety and Miscellaneous plans set required contributions for FY 2026-27 — the same year the campaign's chart identified for the promised payoff and surplus. These figures use CalPERS' funding-basis methodology (market value of assets), distinct from the GASB 68 basis behind the audited Net Pension Liability figures above; both are presented on their own terms. Per CalPERS' June 30, 2024 reports:

  • Combined UAL, both plans, as of 6/30/2024: $25,322,950 ($15,539,980 Safety + $9,782,970 Miscellaneous) — higher than the $15,583,758 combined UAL CalPERS reported for 6/30/2015, the earliest year on file.
  • Combined funded ratio: 73.4% (Safety 71.5%, Miscellaneous 75.9%) — more than a quarter of the liability remains unfunded.
  • Combined required UAL payment, FY 2026-27: $2,545,854, projected by CalPERS to rise — not fall — to a combined $2,999,000 by FY 2031-32.
  • Payoff at minimum required payments: 15.5 years (Safety) and 14.3 years (Miscellaneous) from the FY 2026-27 valuation date — around 2040–2041, roughly three decades after Measure JJ passed, not nine years.
  • CalPERS' current amortization schedule for the Safety plan alone runs to FY 2043-44, with $7,927,851 in interest still projected on that plan's UAL — the opposite of the “$20 million saved” the campaign promised. No public accounting of realized savings, in any form, has been identified in the record reviewed for this document.

A note on what this comparison does not cover — Two figures in the committee's chart — the FY 2025-26 General Fund deficit-to-surplus swing and the reserve trajectory shown on the green and red lines — describe the City's General Fund balance, not the pension liability, and aren't addressed by the CalPERS data above. Verifying those figures would require the City's audited FY 2025-26 financial statements, not yet reviewed for this document. What CalPERS' valuations do establish: the chart's central, headline commitment — “pay off the debt in 9 years” — was not met. Nine years after the promise, the pension debt is larger in dollar terms than CalPERS' own earliest reported figure, and CalPERS' own current schedule does not project payoff for another decade or more.
