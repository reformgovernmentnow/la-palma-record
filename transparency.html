Transparency: Why Residents Couldn't Watch Council Meetings Online

How La Palma cut public meetings in half in 2020, refused video livestreaming for three years, and complied only once state law required it in 2026.

Why this matters

Public meetings are where residents get to address their government on the record. La Palma cut its regular meetings in half in 2020 and never restored them, then spent three years refusing to livestream video — while other Orange County cities, including ones with comparable budgets, did so routinely. The council only acted once a 2026 state law removed its ability to choose otherwise, and even then delayed its own promised start date without advance notice.

Three facts to know

2020: regular meetings were cut from twice-monthly to once monthly, and never restored.
2023–2024: La Palma was 1 of only 2 Orange County cities without livestreaming — the lowest accessibility grade in the county.
2026: streaming was ordered under state law (SB 707) — then delayed to October, announced only a few days before the August 4, 2026 meeting.

The full timeline

Every entry below is drawn from the public record — council resolutions, the City's own statements, and independent reporting. Tap any entry to expand it. Documented marks a claim drawn directly from a primary record, an official's own statement, or a cited investigation.

June 2, 2020Council cuts regular meetings in half, citing COVID — never restores the schedule
Documented — City’s own resolution and later admission

Citing the COVID-19 emergency, the City Council adopts Resolution 2020-29, cutting regular council meetings in half — from the twice-monthly schedule the city had maintained throughout its history to once per month. The City's own later announcement admits the change was made “in an effort to conduct City business during the COVID-19 pandemic” and “has become current practice.” Unlike virtually every other California city that consolidated meetings during COVID, La Palma never restored its normal schedule. The result: the public's regular opportunities to address its government on the record were permanently cut from roughly 24 per year to 12 — and in practice fewer, as the single July meeting is routinely cancelled.

Feb. 7, 2023Council rejects livestreaming — even a no-cost proposal to research it
Quoted opinion — Councilmember Patel

Councilmember Janet Keo Conklin urges the council to consider video livestreaming of public meetings. Councilmembers Nitesh Patel, Mark Waldman, and Debbie Baker oppose it.

The council rejects even a proposal by Councilmember Goodman to have staff merely research streaming options without committing any city funds. Patel acknowledges the city has elderly residents, but says he thinks “there is enough information out there that gets out to them.” Smaller Orange County cities comparable to La Palma in size — including Villa Park, Stanton, and Los Alamitos — already offered video livestreaming of their public meetings at the time.

2023–2024La Palma gets the county's lowest grade for public meeting accessibility
Documented — Voice of OC / Chapman University investigation

A Voice of OC / Chapman University investigation grades all 34 Orange County cities on public meeting accessibility. Every city receives an A or B — except La Palma and Rancho Santa Margarita, the only two cities in the county refusing to livestream video.

Each receives a D.

A First Amendment Coalition legal fellow says the lack of virtual access “keeps residents in the dark.” Then-Councilmember Nitesh Patel told Voice of OC his concerns centered on cost, infrastructure, and how the city would implement it, adding that other cities that already streamed video were larger than La Palma.

Nov. 5, 2024City finds $80,000 for other IT upgrades while still citing cost against streaming
Quoted opinion — Mayor Waldman's own explanation

The same council approves roughly $80,000 in IT upgrades for City Hall — Microsoft Office, a new phone system — while continuing to claim the city cannot afford video streaming. Cities with comparable budgets (Stanton, Los Alamitos) had been streaming for years. Waldman's on-record explanation for the contradiction: “It's different stuff.”

Dec. 2024–Jan. 2025A new agenda rule requires two members' consent — silencing the one member pushing for video

At the council's December 10 meeting, Councilmember Janet Keo Conklin proposes adding video directly — “I propose we now look into [adding] audio and visual,” she says — pointing to Census data showing roughly one in five La Palma residents are of retirement age, and telling Voice of OC afterward that she hoped to bring a proposal forward for greater transparency and accountability. No colleague seconds a further push, and the council instead adopts, 4-1, a “Code of Conduct” requiring two council members' consent to place any item on the public agenda — a rule that structurally silences the lone member who kept raising transparency.

The pattern repeats at the January 14 meeting, where Keo Conklin's motion to direct staff to add video dies again for lack of a second. “So they don't want accountability or transparency. What is wrong with video streaming?” she says afterward.

Mayor Waldman had separately told Voice of OC that few people listen to the audio stream anyway. When she presses the question “what are we hiding?” at the dais, the mayor declares a five-minute recess.

May 2025Professor Samuel Stone: doing the minimum “is not always what everybody wants”
Quoted opinion — Professor Samuel Stone

Voice of OC revisits the story, confirming Keo Conklin's January 14 motion had died for lack of a second from any other member. Samuel Stone, a public administration professor at Fullerton College, offers outside context on the pattern: “Just doing the minimum is not always what everybody wants or expects.”

Apr.–July 2026State law removes the choice; the council directs staff to add video only then
Documented — Voice of OC reporting and the City’s own staff report

Only now — because SB 707, the state's rewrite of the Brown Act effective July 1, 2026, mandates two-way remote public participation — does the council unanimously direct staff to implement video, after Voice of OC had reported that every other Orange County city — all 32 of the others — already offered video livestreaming while La Palma provided audio only. The staff report accompanying the item tells the council that state lawmakers are expected to eventually require video statewide regardless of SB 707.

The city contracts with Western Audio Visual — a vendor and target date Voice of OC later confirmed — projecting the system will be running by August 2026. The council did not act on its own: after three years in which the majority repeatedly refused residents' requests to add video, the change came only because the State of California removed the choice, not because the council chose transparency on its own.

Mayor Patel — who as a councilmember opposed or declined to support the idea in 2023, 2024, and 2025 — now calls the change he resisted for three years “a great thing to do.”

Aug. 3, 2026One day before the meeting, the City announces its own promised system is delayed

One day before the council's regular August 4 meeting, the City posts on its official Facebook page that the video livestreaming system it says the council approved on June 2, 2026 will not be ready as promised. Citing “equipment delays” — with no further detail offered — the post states the City now anticipates video streaming will not begin until the October 6, 2026 meeting, pushing back by at least two months the August 2026 target reported when the council directed staff to pursue the system in April.

The disclosure arrives not as a proactive announcement timed for advance public notice, but as a same-week social media post the day before the very meeting residents might otherwise have expected to be the first livestreamed session. It is the latest instance of a now-familiar pattern in this record: a public-access commitment whose setbacks surface only at the last possible moment.
