Transparency: Why Residents Couldn't Watch Council Meetings Online

How La Palma cut public meetings in half in 2020, refused video livestreaming for three years, and complied only once state law required it in 2026.

Why this matters

Public meetings are where residents get to address their government on the record. La Palma cut its regular meetings in half in 2020 and never restored them, then spent three years refusing to livestream video — while other Orange County cities, including ones with comparable budgets, did so routinely. The council only acted once a 2026 state law removed its ability to choose otherwise, and even then delayed its own promised start date without advance notice.

Three facts to know

Key facts ↓ Full timeline ↓ Source documents ↓
2020: regular meetings were cut from twice-monthly to once monthly, and never restored.
2023–2024: La Palma was 1 of only 2 Orange County cities without livestreaming — the lowest accessibility grade in the county.
2026: streaming was ordered under state law (SB 707) — then delayed to October, announced only a few days before the August 4, 2026 meeting.

The full timeline

Every entry below is drawn from the public record — council resolutions, the City's own statements, and independent reporting. Tap any entry to expand it. Documented marks a claim drawn directly from a primary record, an official's own statement, or a cited investigation.

June 2, 2020Council cuts regular meetings in half, citing COVID — never restores the schedule
Documented — City’s own resolution and later admission

Citing the COVID-19 emergency, the City Council adopts Resolution 2020-29, cutting regular council meetings in half — from the twice-monthly schedule the city had maintained throughout its history to once per month. The City's own later announcement admits the change was made “in an effort to conduct City business during the COVID-19 pandemic” and “has become current practice.” Unlike virtually every other California city that consolidated meetings during COVID, La Palma never restored its normal schedule. The result: the public's regular opportunities to address its government on the record were permanently cut from roughly 24 per year to 12 — and in practice fewer, as the single July meeting is routinely cancelled.

Feb. 7, 2023Council rejects livestreaming — even a no-cost proposal to research it
Quoted opinion — Councilmembers Keo Conklin and Patel

Councilmember Janet Keo Conklin urges the council to consider video livestreaming of public meetings — “We're not in the stone age,” she says. Councilmembers Nitesh Patel, Mark Waldman, and Debbie Baker oppose it.

The council rejects even a proposal by Councilmember Goodman to have staff merely research streaming options without committing any city funds. Patel acknowledges the city has elderly residents, but says he thinks “there is enough information out there that gets out to them.” Smaller Orange County cities comparable to La Palma in size — including Villa Park, Stanton, and Los Alamitos — already offered video livestreaming of their public meetings at the time.

Mar. 8, 2023Voice of OC's report on the vote: audio-only research approved 3-2, a broader video-research motion fails first

Voice of OC's published account of the February 7 meeting adds detail the agenda item alone doesn't capture. Before the council took up audio improvements, Councilmembers Janet Keo Conklin and Marshall Goodman first offered a substitute motion asking staff to research the cost of improving both audio quality and adding video livestreaming. That broader motion failed on a council majority vote.

Only after the video-research option was rejected did the council vote 3-2 to research audio-quality improvements alone — with Keo Conklin and Goodman voting against that narrower motion, having wanted the broader one to pass instead. Councilmember Mark Waldman explained his opposition to video by citing “grandstanding” at a prior council that had considered it: “some people come in to hear themselves speak... they just want to come and perform.” La Palma resident Bonnie Knartzer, then 75, told the paper she didn't understand the reluctance: “I do have a hearing problem of sorts... why not just add one extra step?”

Feb. 21, 2024La Palma gets a "D" — the county's lowest grade for public meeting accessibility
Documented — Voice of OC / Chapman University investigation

A Voice of OC / Chapman University investigation grades all 34 Orange County cities on public meeting accessibility. Every city receives an A or B — except La Palma and Rancho Santa Margarita, the only two cities in the county refusing to livestream video.

Each receives a D.

A First Amendment Coalition legal fellow says the lack of virtual access “keeps residents in the dark.” Then-Councilmember Nitesh Patel told Voice of OC his concerns centered on cost, infrastructure, and how the city would implement it, adding that other cities that already streamed video were larger than La Palma.

Nov. 5, 2024City finds $80,000 for other IT upgrades while still citing cost against streaming
Quoted opinion — Mayor Waldman's own explanation

The same council approves roughly $80,000 in IT upgrades for City Hall — Microsoft Office, a new phone system — while continuing to claim the city cannot afford video streaming. Cities with comparable budgets (Stanton, Los Alamitos) had been streaming for years. Waldman's on-record explanation for the contradiction: “It's different stuff.”

Dec. 10, 2024New agenda rule stops video proposal one month later

At the council's December 10 meeting, Councilmember Janet Keo Conklin proposes adding video directly — “I propose we now look into [adding] audio and visual,” she says — pointing to Census data showing roughly one in five La Palma residents are of retirement age, and telling Voice of OC afterward that she hoped to bring a proposal forward for greater transparency and accountability. No colleague seconds a further push, and the council instead adopts, 4-1, a “Code of Conduct” requiring two council members' consent to place any item on the public agenda.

The pattern repeats at the January 14 meeting, where Keo Conklin's motion to direct staff to add video dies again for lack of a second. “So they don't want accountability or transparency. What is wrong with video streaming?” she says afterward.

Mayor Waldman had separately told Voice of OC he saw no reason to change course: “I don't see the need for it,” he said, adding that few people listen to the audio stream anyway, though he'd “be all for it” if residents really wanted it — he just didn't “see the point.” Councilmembers Debbie Baker and Nitesh Patel did not respond to the same reporter's questions. When Keo Conklin presses the question “what are we hiding?” at the dais, the mayor declares a five-minute recess.

May 6, 2025Professor Samuel Stone: doing the minimum “is not always what everybody wants”
Quoted opinion — Councilmembers Keo Conklin, Waldman, and Patel

Voice of OC revisits the story after the council's April 1, 2025 meeting, confirming Keo Conklin's January 14 motion had died for lack of a second from any other member. Keo Conklin tells the paper her position hasn't changed: “My core value is transparency and accountability,” citing moments during staff presentations when online viewers couldn't see items projected onto the chamber screen, and adding that she has nothing to hide.

Mayor Waldman again saw no need to change course: “We speak and test our votes verbally,” he said, arguing video wouldn't increase transparency. Nitesh Patel pointed to cost: “The cost is exorbitant,” he said, adding, “When we bring something that is $100,000 or $150,000 to implement, people are going to scream at us and yell at us, saying, ‘Well, that's too expensive.’” Councilmembers Vikesh Patel and Debbie Baker did not respond to the reporter's requests for comment.

Resident Robert Carruth — a local watchdog since 2010 — asked why the city continued to lag behind its neighbors: “Why are we not doing what every other city except for Rancho Santa Margarita does, including small cities like Los Alamitos, Villa Park and Stanton?” Samuel Stone, a public administration professor at Fullerton College, offered outside context on the pattern: “Just doing the minimum is not always what everybody wants or expects.”

Oct. 3, 2025Sacramento passes SB 707, requiring video access to public meetings by July 1, 2026
Documented — signed legislation

Governor Newsom signs SB 707 on October 3, 2025, the state's rewrite of the Brown Act. Effective July 1, 2026, the law mandates two-way remote public participation at meetings of covered legislative bodies, including La Palma's City Council — removing the discretion the council had exercised for years.

Apr. 7, 2026After State law removes the choice, the council directs staff to implement video
Documented — Voice of OC reporting and the City’s own staff report

The council had known about SB 707's requirements since October 3, 2025, when the law was signed, and knew the deadline for compliance was July 1, 2026. It waited another six months — until its April 7, 2026 meeting — before directing staff to research and implement a solution.

Only now — with the SB 707 deadline approaching — does the council unanimously direct staff to implement video, after Voice of OC had reported that every other Orange County city — all 32 of the others — already offered video livestreaming while La Palma provided audio only. The staff report accompanying the item tells the council that state lawmakers are expected to eventually require video statewide regardless of SB 707.

The city contracts with Western Audio Visual — a vendor and target date Voice of OC later confirmed — projecting the system will be running by August 2026. The sequence suggests the change was driven by the new state requirement rather than a shift in the council's own position: after three years in which the majority repeatedly refused residents' requests to add video, video access moved forward only after SB 707 removed the council's discretion to decline it.

Mayor Patel — who as a councilmember opposed or declined to support the idea in 2023, 2024, and 2025 — now calls the change he resisted for three years “a great thing to do.”

Aug. 3, 2026One day before the meeting, the City announces its own promised system is delayed

One day before the council's regular August 4 meeting, the City posts on its official Facebook page that the video livestreaming system it says the council approved on June 2, 2026 will not be ready as promised. Citing “equipment delays” — with no further detail offered — the post states the City now anticipates video streaming will not begin until the October 6, 2026 meeting, pushing back by at least two months the August 2026 target reported when the council directed staff to pursue the system in April.

The disclosure arrives not as a proactive announcement timed for advance public notice, but as a same-week social media post the day before the very meeting residents might otherwise have expected to be the first livestreamed session. It is the latest instance of a now-familiar pattern in this record: a public-access commitment whose setbacks surface only at the last possible moment.
